\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Since 2024 a growing body of empirical work has estimated how much of open-source
software is written by AI. The results are reported as percentages of commits, pull
requests, comments or lines of code, and they are used to argue about maintainer burden,
review overhead, contribution quality and project sustainability. Few of these estimates
rest on a measurement instrument that has been validated on the kind of text it is
applied to.

Three families of instrument are in use. Zero-shot statistical detectors such as
DetectGPT \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt}, Fast-DetectGPT \citep{bao2024fastdetectgpt} and
Binoculars \citep{hans2024binoculars} score text by its likelihood under a language
model. Heuristic filters match phrase lists or formatting signatures. Large language
models are prompted to classify authorship directly. All three were developed and
benchmarked on news articles, student essays and curated programming benchmarks.
Binoculars, the strongest published claim, reports detection of over \citednum{90\%} of
ChatGPT-generated text at a false positive rate of \citednum{0.01\%}
\citep{hans2024binoculars}, measured on essay-length prose.

Open-source artifacts are not essay-length prose. In the AIDev dataset of agent-authored
contributions \citep{li2025aidev}, the median commit message is
\AidevCommitMsgMedian{} characters and the ninetieth percentile is
\AidevCommitMsgPNinety{}; the median pull request body is \AidevBodyMedian{} characters.
These texts are short, follow genre conventions, and are written by a globally
distributed contributor base, most of whom write English as a second language. Each of
these properties is a known difficulty for the detectors in question. Accuracy falls on
short inputs, and \citet{liang2023biased} found that detectors classify writing by
non-native English speakers as machine-generated at elevated rates. Open-source
repositories also contain a decade of text produced by dependency bots, release
automation and issue templates, all of which predate large language models and none of
which published studies have been shown to exclude reliably.

Measurement error of this kind does not average out. Non-AI artifacts outnumber AI ones
by a wide margin in historical samples, so even a small false positive rate produces
many false detections and biases estimates upward rather than adding noise. The bias
reported by \citet{liang2023biased} would fall on contributors who are already least
well served by the ecosystem.

\subsection{Auditing the instrument}
\label{sec:intro:inversion}

This paper asks what the existing instruments measure when applied to open-source
artifacts, and what follows for the estimates already published. Two sources of ground
truth make the question tractable without human annotation, which matters because
agreement on whether a text looks AI-written is itself what is in dispute.

Agentic coding platforms leave attribution traces. When Devin, OpenAI Codex, GitHub
Copilot, Cursor or Claude Code opens a pull request under its own account, authorship is
declared by the platform rather than inferred. AIDev collects \AidevPRs{} such pull
requests across \AidevRepos{} repositories, and \AidevTrailerRate{} of the commits in
that dataset carry a \texttt{Co-authored-by} trailer, a second declared channel.

Text written before large language models were publicly available cannot have been
produced by them. Artifacts committed to the same repositories before the release of
ChatGPT on \LlmCutoff{} therefore form a negative set whose composition follows from
chronology rather than judgement. Any artifact a detector flags in this set is a false
positive by construction, so the resulting rate is measured rather than estimated under
assumptions.

Given sensitivity and specificity, published figures can then be corrected.
\citet{rogan1978estimating} solved the corresponding problem for screening tests in
epidemiology in 1978. Applying that correction requires exactly the quantities that have
not been measured for this domain, which is what Section~\ref{sec:validity} provides.

\subsection{Why this matters now}
\label{sec:intro:now}

The 2026 Mining Software Repositories Mining Challenge was built on AIDev and produced
\citednum{62} accepted papers on merge and rejection rates, test coverage, continuous
integration outcomes, security defects, refactoring behaviour, code clones and reviewer
sentiment in agent-authored contributions. All of them treat the attribution labels as
given, which is reasonable since those labels are platform-declared. A research
programme has been built on authorship attribution in under two years, while what
attribution by detection measures, in the common case where declaration is absent, has
not been examined.

\citet{khosravani2026census} examine the declaration channels themselves, including
configuration files, commit-message markers, author identity and bot signatures, across
\citednum{180} million repositories. They find that single-signal detection undercounts
badly: bot-account lookup alone recovers \citednum{3.3\%} of the Claude Code commits
their multi-method approach identifies. Their result and ours point in opposite
directions. They show that declaration-based measurement misses real AI contributions;
we ask whether content-based measurement reports contributions that are not there.
Together the two bound an estimate from both sides.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A criterion-validity evaluation of the detection approaches used in the
    open-source literature, against attribution-based ground truth, reported per agent
    and per artifact genre (Section~\ref{sec:validity}). Reporting per agent is
    necessary because \AidevTopAgentShare{} of AIDev is a single agent, so a pooled
    metric describes that agent rather than agents in general.
  \item A false positive measurement on pre-ChatGPT artifacts, where every detection is
    an error by construction, stratified by genre, language, text length and project
    characteristics, and separated from the contribution of pre-LLM automation
    (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).
  \item Corrected estimates that propagate the measured error rates through the
    Rogan--Gladen estimator, reported as intervals under best- and worst-case detector
    assumptions and set against the point estimates in the literature
    (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).
  \item A provenance model that represents an authorship claim together with its
    evidence type, whether a self-declared trailer, platform metadata, an agent account,
    or a detector score, so that attributions can be queried and inspected
    (Section~\ref{sec:provenance}).
  \item A replication package containing the corpus specification, the detector harness,
    the provenance vocabulary and all analysis code.
\end{enumerate}

We do not claim that AI-generated content in open source is rare, or that the concerns
motivating this literature are misplaced. The claim is narrower: the field cannot
currently separate a detector responding to AI text from one responding to short text,
conventional text, or text written by a non-native speaker. That is measurable, and it
is correctable.
