\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A literature has grown up around measuring how much of open-source software is written
by AI, and a further literature builds on those measurements. This paper asked what the
measuring instruments do when applied to open-source artifacts, using ground truth that
needs no human annotation: contributions agents are declared to have written, and
artifacts committed to the same repositories before ChatGPT existed.

The answer is specific. Across \ZeroShotCells{} combinations of zero-shot detector and
artifact genre, discrimination falls below chance in every one, so these instruments
rank pre-ChatGPT human writing as more machine-written than agent output. A supervised
classifier given the same text and nothing else reaches \CeilingAurocMax{} on pull
request bodies, so the signal exists in at least one genre and the deployed instruments
are not finding it. An instructed language model flags pre-ChatGPT automation between
\JudgeBotFprMin{} and \JudgeBotFprMax{} per cent of the time while calibrated to five
per cent on human text from the same period, which makes it a detector of machine
authorship in general. The keyword scans two of the studies rely on carry no false
positives and almost no recall. Propagating these rates through the Rogan--Gladen
correction leaves \CorrectionUndefined{} of \CorrectionCells{} strata with an undefined
estimate and \CorrectionOutOfRange{} more with an observed rate that no prevalence in
the unit interval could have produced.

The corrections are cheap where they are possible: validate on the genre being counted,
report per stratum, separate pre-language-model automation, state the operating point
with its measured error, and prefer declared attribution to inferred attribution
wherever both exist. To make the last of these usable we give \texttt{aiprov}, a small
PROV-O extension that records an authorship claim together with its evidence type and,
for detector evidence, the measured error rate of the stratum it came from.

None of this argues that AI-generated content in open source is rare or that the
concerns motivating the literature are misplaced. The claim is narrower and, we think,
prior to those questions: a field that reports prevalence should be able to say how well
it can measure it, and on the evidence assembled here it currently cannot.
